This comes down to a phonetic phenomenon rather than these words being true homophones. In English, "sue" and "zoo" are distinguished mainly by the voicing of their initial consonant: /s/ is voiceless, while /z/ is voiced. This is a real, meaningful contrast in English, as shown by minimal pairs like "sip" versus "zip," where swapping the sound changes the meaning of the word entirely.

Many non-native speakers, particularly those whose first language doesn't have this same contrast, sometimes struggle to consistently produce or perceive the difference. Spanish, for example, only has a single alveolar fricative phoneme, essentially always realized as something close to /s/, so Spanish speakers may not automatically distinguish /s/ and /z/ the way English speakers do, since their native phonology doesn't require it.

Interestingly, there's a related nuance even among native English speakers. At the end of words, voiced consonants like /z/ often become partially or fully devoiced, especially when not immediately followed by another voiced sound. This means that in casual, natural speech, the "z" sound at the end of a word like "was" or "has" may sound much closer to an /s/ than people realize, even though native speakers usually insist they can hear a clear "z." If you isolate just that final sound and play it back without the rest of the word, listeners often misidentify it as an /s/, which shows how much context and expectation shape our perception of sounds.

So while "sue" and "zoo" remain phonemically distinct in English, and are not homophones by any formal definition, the actual acoustic difference between voiced and voiceless sounds is often smaller and more context-dependent than most speakers assume.
