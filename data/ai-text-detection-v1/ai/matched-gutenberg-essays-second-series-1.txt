Genius, in its truest sense, is not a possession but an activity, a continual renewal that pushes against the decay inherent in all finite things. Nature herself seems to operate on this same principle at every scale, from the smallest organism to the grandest ecosystem. She does not linger over any single failure; where one seed perishes, a thousand more are scattered in its place, indifferent to the fate of any particular one, so long as the pattern continues.

This same restless energy characterizes the truly original mind. It does not rest content with what has already been achieved, however admirable, but is compelled to search further, to test old truths against new circumstances, and to discard comfortable certainties the moment they cease to illuminate anything new. The danger, of course, is that this same restlessness can just as easily become mere agitation, movement without direction, if it is not anchored by some deeper fidelity to what is real and true.

We often mistake the trappings of genius, eccentricity, isolation, a disregard for convention, for the substance of it. But these are at best incidental, and at worst a kind of costume worn by those who lack the thing itself. True originality is not loud. It does not announce itself through mere difference from others; it reveals itself through depth, through the capacity to see freshly into what everyone else has stopped looking at closely.

In this sense, genius is less a rare gift bestowed upon a few than a discipline available, in some measure, to anyone willing to keep looking, keep questioning, and refuse the comfortable fiction that any truth, once found, need never be examined again.
