The Golden Gate Bridge is a suspension bridge that spans the Golden Gate strait, the narrow passage connecting San Francisco Bay to the Pacific Ocean. Completed in 1937, it links the city of San Francisco to Marin County to the north and remains one of the most recognizable feats of engineering in the world.

At the time of its completion, the Golden Gate Bridge held the title of the longest suspension bridge span on Earth, a record it maintained for nearly three decades. Its total length is just over 2.7 kilometers, and its two main towers rise more than 220 meters above the water, a height that made construction especially challenging given the strong currents, frequent fog, and high winds common to the area. Chief engineer Joseph Strauss led the project, though much of the bridge's elegant design is credited to architect Irving Morrow, who chose its now-famous "International Orange" color both for visibility in fog and for aesthetic harmony with the surrounding landscape.

Construction took about four years and was considered remarkably safe for its era, thanks in part to strict safety measures introduced by Strauss, including a large safety net suspended beneath the work area that is credited with saving the lives of numerous workers during the build.

Today, the bridge carries both vehicle traffic and pedestrians, and it remains a vital transportation link as well as one of the most photographed landmarks in the United States. It has been designated one of the Wonders of the Modern World by the American Society of Civil Engineers, and it continues to be a defining symbol of San Francisco, appearing prominently in countless films, postcards, and works of art depicting the city.
