This is a really interesting catch, and I think the explanation comes down to the ball likely being designed with more than one language in mind, possibly Scandinavian.

If you look closely at the letter-picture pairings, most of them work perfectly well in English: A for apple, B for bear, C for cat, and so on, these are all intuitive, common words a young child would recognize immediately. But a few pairings stand out as unusual choices for an English-speaking audience, and those are the ones worth focusing on.

The clearest example is likely the U pairing. In everyday English, "submarine" is by far the more common word a toddler would use, "U-boat" is a term mostly associated with World War II history and isn't typically part of a young child's vocabulary at all. However, in several Scandinavian languages, including Swedish, the word for submarine is closer to "u-båt," which maps naturally onto the letter U in a way it simply doesn't in English.

Checking a few other pairings against Swedish vocabulary tends to support this theory. Words like "björn" (bear) for B and "hus" (house) for H line up reasonably well, while a handful of others don't quite fit as neatly, which suggests the manufacturer may have adapted a template originally created for a different language market, or possibly translated the concept without native-level review.

It's a great example of how children's educational products sometimes get produced for a broad international market, with the letter-object pairings translated instead of separately optimized for each language, occasionally leading to slightly mismatched results like this one.
