The Ganges is one of the most significant rivers in the world, both geographically and culturally. Flowing for roughly 2,500 kilometers, it originates in the Himalayas and travels southeast through northern India before crossing into Bangladesh, where it eventually empties into the Bay of Bengal. Along the way, it passes through some of the most densely populated regions on Earth, supporting the lives of hundreds of millions of people.

For Hindus, the Ganges is not just a river but a sacred entity, personified as the goddess Ganga. Bathing in its waters is considered a purifying act, and the river plays a central role in numerous religious rites, from daily worship to funeral rituals. Cities along its banks, such as Varanasi and Haridwar, are major pilgrimage destinations, drawing millions of visitors every year who come to perform ceremonies on its ghats.

Historically, the river has shaped the political and cultural landscape of the Indian subcontinent. Its fertile floodplain, known as the Gangetic Plain, has supported agriculture for thousands of years and was home to several ancient and medieval capitals, including Kannauj and Patna. The river also served as a vital trade and transportation route long before modern infrastructure existed.

Unfortunately, the Ganges faces severe environmental challenges today. Decades of industrial discharge, untreated sewage, and agricultural runoff have left large stretches of the river heavily polluted, threatening both public health and the diverse wildlife that depends on it, including endangered species like the Ganges river dolphin and the gharial crocodile. Government-led cleanup efforts, most notably the Namami Gange programme, have made some progress, but experts generally agree that pollution levels remain far above safe limits in many areas.

Despite these challenges, the Ganges continues to be revered as a lifeline, both spiritually and materially, for the communities that live along its course.
