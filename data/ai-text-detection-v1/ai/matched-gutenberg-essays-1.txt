Simulation, the active pretense of being something one is not, deserves to be judged more harshly than mere secrecy, which only withholds what one truly is. To conceal is defensible in many circumstances; to actively construct a false impression is a different matter, and ought to be reserved, if used at all, for rare and weighty occasions rather than habitual practice.

Still, there are advantages that make simulation tempting, and it is worth naming them honestly rather than pretending virtue alone governs human affairs. First, it can catch an opponent off guard, since a plainly stated intention invites resistance before it can be carried out, while a well-concealed one proceeds unchallenged. Second, it preserves room to retreat: a person who has committed openly to a course of action has no graceful way back, while one who has kept their true aim hidden may withdraw without visible defeat. Third, and perhaps most usefully, it can draw out the true intentions of others, since people tend to speak more freely and reveal more of themselves to someone who appears open, even when that openness is itself a kind of performance.

But these advantages come at a real cost. A reputation for simulation, once earned, corrodes the very trust that makes cooperation and negotiation possible. Even allies begin to wonder what is being concealed from them, and the habitual dissembler eventually finds that no one will extend the benefit of the doubt, precisely when it is needed most.

The wiser course, then, is not perpetual openness, which leaves one perpetually exposed, nor habitual deception, which eventually isolates its practitioner, but a disciplined honesty of character paired with a prudent, occasional reticence about one's specific plans.
