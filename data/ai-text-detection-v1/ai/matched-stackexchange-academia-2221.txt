This feeling is incredibly common, and honestly, it sounds like you're already doing the right thing even though it doesn't feel like it yet: you're still showing up and still trying, even when progress feels invisible.

Research, almost by definition, involves spending most of your time on problems that don't yield, at least at first. If a problem were easy or obvious, it probably wouldn't be worth publishing. So a huge part of the experience, especially early in a research career, is repeatedly running into walls that don't move, at least not for a while.

A few things that might help reframe this. First, it's genuinely useful to be working on more than one problem at a time, if one direction stalls completely, having another avenue to make progress on can prevent the discouragement from becoming totally paralyzing. Second, it's worth recognizing that a lot of "breakthroughs" in research aren't really sudden flashes of brilliance, they're the result of a long accumulation of failed attempts that quietly narrowed down what doesn't work, until eventually something does.

Also, that voice telling you that "someone smarter would have solved this by now" is something nearly every researcher hears, even the ones who seem effortlessly accomplished from the outside. Impostor syndrome doesn't really go away because you get smarter, it usually fades (if it fades at all) because you slowly accumulate enough evidence, papers, successful problems, milestones like earning tenure, that eventually your track record starts to outweigh the doubt, even if the doubt never fully disappears.

It gets easier to trust that persistence pays off. It doesn't necessarily get less frustrating in the moment.
