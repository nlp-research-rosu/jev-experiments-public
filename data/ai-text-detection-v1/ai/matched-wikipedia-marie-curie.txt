Marie Curie remains one of the most extraordinary figures in the history of science. Born Maria Skłodowska in Warsaw in 1867, she grew up under Russian occupation at a time when Polish institutions restricted higher education for women. Determined to pursue science, she attended the underground "Flying University" before moving to Paris in 1891 to study at the Sorbonne, where she immersed herself in physics and mathematics, often living in near poverty to fund her studies.

Her scientific breakthroughs are almost impossible to overstate. Working alongside her husband, Pierre Curie, she pioneered research into radioactivity, a term she herself coined. Together they discovered two new elements, **polonium** and **radium**, transforming scientists' understanding of atomic structure. In 1903, she became the first woman to win a Nobel Prize, sharing the Physics prize with Pierre and Henri Becquerel. Astonishingly, she won a second Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1911, making her the only person in history to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.

Curie's contributions went far beyond the laboratory. During World War I, she developed mobile X-ray units, nicknamed "Little Curies," which she personally helped operate near the front lines to assist battlefield surgeons. She also founded the Curie Institutes in Paris and Warsaw, institutions that remain leading centers for cancer research to this day.

Tragically, her pioneering work came at a steep personal cost. Prolonged exposure to radiation, at a time when its dangers were not yet understood, led to her death in 1934 from aplastic anemia. Even now, some of her papers and personal belongings are so radioactive that they must be stored in lead-lined boxes.

Marie Curie's legacy endures not just in her scientific discoveries, but as a symbol of perseverance against overwhelming odds, breaking barriers for women in science that still inspire researchers today.
