so this comes down to which grape we're actually talking about. most "grape flavored" stuff in the US isn't based on regular table grapes or wine grapes at all, it's based on Concord grapes, which are a totally different species, Vitis labrusca instead of Vitis vinifera. table grapes and wine grapes are basically the same species, just different varieties, but Concord grapes have this distinct musky flavor that's totally different from what you'd get biting into a green seedless grape from the store.

that musky flavor mostly comes down to one chemical, methyl anthranilate, and it happens to be pretty cheap and easy to synthesize. so when a company wants to slap "grape flavor" on a candy or a soda, they're not extracting anything from actual grapes, they're just using that one compound, because it's inexpensive and instantly recognizable as "grape" to anyone who grew up in North America.

the thing is, actual wine grapes and table grapes don't really have one single dominant flavor compound like that, their taste comes from a much more complex mix, so there's no cheap synthetic shortcut the way there is with Concord grapes. you could theoretically try to recreate that more subtle flavor artificially, but it'd cost way more and probably still wouldn't taste quite right to people.

and honestly there's a cultural loop here too. because grape candy has tasted like Concord grapes forever, that's just what people expect "grape" to taste like now, even though it's nothing like the grapes most of us actually eat fresh. so even if some company made a wine-grape-flavored candy, a lot of people would probably say it just tastes wrong.
