Yeah, this is a pretty common installation mistake, and it will cause problems over time if it's not corrected.

What should have been done is running the downspout from the upper roof section all the way down to the lower roof using proper transition and elbow fittings, so the water discharges directly into the lower gutter instead of just spilling out onto the shingles partway down.

Letting water dump straight onto the roof surface, even if it's angled to follow the slope, causes a concentrated flow that wears down the shingles much faster than normal rainfall would. Water naturally sheets off a roof in a fairly even distribution, but a downspout concentrates a much larger volume into one small area, so you end up with accelerated granule loss and premature wear right where that water lands, and it only gets worse over time as the channel it wears into the shingles gets deeper.

It's a frustrating shortcut, honestly, because doing it correctly with an extension and elbow really isn't that expensive or difficult, it's mostly just a matter of the installer not wanting to spend the extra time or materials. But the long-term cost is a section of roof that wears out well before the rest, which usually ends up costing more to repair than the few dollars saved during installation.

If you're dealing with this on your own roof, it's worth having a roofer extend the downspout properly rather than leaving it as is, especially if you're noticing granule buildup or bare patches forming under where the water currently discharges.
