**The core issue: your incentive structure sent the wrong message**

It sounds like the message your employee actually received, whether or not it was the one you intended to send, was that time in the role matters more than the quality of the work or the skills brought to it. If that's the explicit or implicit standard for promotion at your company, it's not surprising that he responded by optimizing for exactly that metric: staying in his seat longer, rather than continuing to invest extra effort or take on additional learning that apparently wasn't being rewarded.

A few things worth considering:

- If overtime or self-directed learning outside of work hours is genuinely expected for advancement, that expectation needs to be made explicit, ideally in writing, rather than assumed.
- Simply meeting the requirements of one's contract is not, on its own, a legitimate reason for termination in most jurisdictions with reasonable labor protections.
- It's worth asking yourself honestly: if this employee applied for a director-level role from outside the company, with the skills and results they currently have, would you consider them? If yes, the internal promotion criteria may need revisiting. If no, that gap should be communicated clearly so they have a real chance to address it.

Right now, it sounds like there's a mismatch between what was said and what was rewarded, and unfortunately, both sides may remember this tension going forward. The most productive path is probably a direct, non-accusatory conversation about what's actually required for advancement, rather than assuming the employee is simply being difficult by doing what the position formally requires.
